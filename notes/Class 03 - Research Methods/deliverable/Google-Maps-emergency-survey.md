# Google Maps emergency routing: my three survey questions

## The case that started it

In class we heard about a family in Sarjapur, Bangalore. The mother had a seizure, and the ambulance was booked through Google Maps. Maps sent it to the hospital's main gate, which was on the far side. The family always used the back gate, five minutes from home. The detour took 30 minutes, and she was left with lasting paralysis on one side. The son took Google Maps to the Karnataka High Court.

The homework: if I were solving Google Maps emergency routing, what survey questions would I ask?

My first instinct was to ask "Would an emergency mode help you?" Everyone says yes to that, and it tells me nothing. So I started from the decision instead.

## What I want to decide

Should Google Maps guide people to the right emergency entrance of a hospital, and not just to the hospital's address?

I kept this narrow on purpose. "A hospital that can actually help" is a different problem, and this survey doesn't test it (see the end).

## Who I'm asking

People in Indian cities who went to a hospital in a hurry in the last 12 months, either driving or as a passenger. One question filters them first:

> In the last 12 months, have you had to get to a hospital urgently, for yourself or someone else?
> Yes · No · Prefer not to say

Only those who say Yes see the three questions.

## My three questions

**1. On that trip, did anyone use a map app to find the way, including the driver or the ambulance?**

- Yes, I did
- Yes, the driver or ambulance did
- No
- Don't know
- Prefer not to say

I first asked "who decided the route", but an ambulance driver can decide the route using Google Maps. So this question only asks whether a map app guided the trip, whoever was holding the phone.

**2. Which of these slowed you down? Select all that apply.**

- Traffic
- Working out which hospital to go to
- Finding the emergency entrance once we got there
- The hospital couldn't help us
- Parking
- Nothing slowed us down
- Prefer not to say

I first made people pick one. That hides problems: a 15-minute hunt for the entrance disappears behind a 10-minute traffic jam. Selecting all tells me whether each problem happened at all, which is what I need to count.

**3. Did you reach the right entrance on the first try?**

- Yes, straight away
- Yes, but only after asking someone
- No, we went to the wrong gate or building first
- I don't remember
- Prefer not to say

This is the Sarjapur problem, asked directly.

## How I'll read the answers

I won't decide by what most people say. Most people may know the way to their nearest hospital, and that says nothing about the families who didn't. In an emergency, a rare failure can still be a serious one.

So I'll look at one group: people who used a map app (Q1) and also struggled to find the entrance (Q2) or went to the wrong gate first (Q3).

| What I find | What I do next |
|---|---|
| Even a small group like this | Interview them to understand how bad it was and how much time it cost |
| Hardly anyone in this group, but a lot of "finding which hospital" or "hospital couldn't help" | The problem is choosing the hospital, not the gate. Research that next |
| Traffic is the only common delay | Routing speed matters more than entrances |

The survey tells me how often the problem happens. The interviews tell me how serious it is. I need both before deciding anything.

## What this survey doesn't cover

There are really three problems in emergency routing, and this survey mostly tests the first:

| Problem | What would solve it |
|---|---|
| Maps sends people to the wrong entrance | Verified emergency entrance directions |
| The hospital can't treat this emergency | Reliable information on what each hospital can handle |
| The hospital could treat it but can't take the patient right now | Up-to-date availability information |

Showing a positive result on entrances would not be enough to justify a full emergency mode. The second and third problems need their own research.

## What I took from this

Every question asks about one real trip, not what people think they'd do. None of them mentions an emergency mode, so nobody can guess the answer I want.

The bigger lesson came from having my first draft challenged. I had treated "most people" as the answer, but for a safety problem, how serious a failure is matters as much as how often it happens. A survey can count. It can't tell you how bad it was.

Someone answering this may be remembering one of the worst days of their life. That's why the wording stays plain and calm, and why every question lets them skip.
